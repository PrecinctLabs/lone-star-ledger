The State's Count vs. the State's Own Roster

The Secretary of State publishes an in-person early-vote total for each county. The state also publishes a roster — the actual list of people it says voted in person there. We count the names on the state's own roster and set them against the state's own total. Both numbers come from the state. No county data is involved.

This compares the state only to itself. If the state's headline number for a county is right, it should be able to name that many in-person voters. A positive gap means the total claims more votes than the state's roster can put a name to — partly the roster filling in a day behind the total, partly voters the state counts but does not list. A negative gap means the roster names more people than the total admits voted. A gap near zero means the state agrees with itself. The gap is timed only to the window between two of our captures.

How to read this

  • State total is the Secretary of State's Civix early-voting feed — the total_in_person_votes_for_election it publishes for the county, which the feed describes as votes. State roster is the count of distinct voters on the state's own per-day in-person rosters for that county.
  • A positive gap (total > roster) is the common case during voting: a day's roster is posted a little behind the total, and some share never resolves — voters the state counts but cannot name. A negative gap (roster > total) means the state lists more people than its own total says voted — the headline is lower than the names behind it.
  • A gap near zero — Bexar, Cameron, El Paso, Randall here — is the state being internally consistent, and it shows the check is not simply manufacturing a gap: where the state agrees with itself, we report that.
  • Hays is sampled a few times a day (its county site blocks automated access), so its figure moves in coarser steps than the rest — but both sides are still the state's.
  • Why it matters: a county's own books are a separate question (that is the main dashboard). This page sets that aside and asks only whether the state's two public records of the same election agree with each other.

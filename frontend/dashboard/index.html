Voter Counts: Counties vs. Secretary of State

12 Texas counties publish their own early-vote rosters. The Secretary of State publishes a second set of figures for the same counties. We fetch both every 15 minutes, keep every version, and match them voter by voter — so we can see, name by name, which early voters a county has already recorded that the state's data has not caught up to. That difference is a backlog, the downstream of failures in Texas' election systems that were flagged two years ago and never fixed. We don't predict whether it clears. We show it as it stands, and the final answer will come only when the state declares its backlog processed and we run the numbers then — a fact, not a forecast.

02 · The backlog

The Backlog, Over Time

The backlog is every early voter a county has already recorded on its own roster that the state's data has not matched yet, summed across all matched counties, plotted at each hourly snapshot since we began tracking. Each evening a county posts a fresh early-vote day and the line jumps; over the following day the state matches those voters and it falls. We are finding that county voters in the backlog do eventually appear in the state's data — but this is not the whole backlog, and whether the line reaches zero is a question only the next few days can answer. We make no prediction: early voting closed Oct 30, so from here no new days are added, and the line now shows whether the state drains what remains.

Collin, Denton and Randall joined the voter-by-voter match on Oct 30, so the line steps up that day — that is counties added, not backlog grown. Watch the trend from the close of early voting forward.

    Voter by Voter: A Closer Look at the Gap

    The state says they voted — the county has no such record. This is the state-only direction, and it is the one that matters most: as of Oct 30 the state lists 519 early voters in Travis that Travis's own roster does not. We pulled this thread in Travis because it is the one county where we hold the full registration file with voting history. Of those 519 we examined the 259 on in-person days the state has finished posting (the rest are the newest days, where the county simply has not published yet), and looked each one up.

    04 · Who we can read

    Party Lean and the Large Blind Spot

    Texas has no party registration, so the only signal for party is which party's primary a voter has chosen in the past. For the two counties where we have that history, this is the make-up of the people who have voted early so far. The grey band is the catch: a quarter to a third of early voters have never voted in a primary, so we cannot assign them a lean at all.

    Voted in a Democratic primary Voted in a Republican primary No primary history (no lean)

    Because the grey band is so large, no partisan read of these numbers speaks for the whole electorate. It describes only the voters we can classify, and that is how it should be quoted.

    05 · How we manage voter data

    How We Manage Voter Data: Numbers, Archives, and Privacy

    Two Independent Counts

    Counties build their totals from the sign-in books at polling places and their mail-ballot logs. The state builds its "Early Voting Report" from what counties upload to the statewide registration system. The two travel on different schedules, so a gap that closes in a day is normal. A gap that persists, or a past day that keeps changing, is the reason this site was created.

    Our Data Archive

    We check all sources every 15 minutes and save a new copy only when the contents change. Several counties overwrite voter-data, so a version we fail to capture is gone for good. Keeping every version is key in discovering revisions to the voter-data.

    Names Remain Private

    The voter-by-voter matching happens offline. This page and anything we publish carry counts only. No voter names or ID numbers are shown, logged, or shared.
